Hello everyone,

A short note on how multi-star alignment works in 1.6, what changed relative to 1.5, and why the math looks the way it does. This is meant as a practical overview for the group; the full detail stays in the field manuals and in docs/math/alignment.md on GitHub.

What 1.5 already did

Version 1.5 used a two-star alignment (hand controller or plate-solve syncs). From two measured directions it builds a single 3×3 rotation matrix (Taki’s construction, then the nearest proper rotation in the least-squares sense). That matrix maps the sky to the mount axes for pointing, sync and corrected tracking. It is a solid model when the mount geometry is close to ideal.

What it does not separate out are the usual head errors — collimation of the optical axis, non-perpendicularity of the two axes, and a declination index. Those effects, if present, get absorbed partly into the estimated pole, which can leave systematic residuals across the sky or after a pier flip.

What 1.6 adds

The two-star path is still there and behaves as before when no head errors are held. On top of that, the hand controller now guides longer sessions:

  • 4 Stars — four stars on the same pier side. Used mainly to measure NP (non-perpendicularity).
  • 3+3 Stars — three stars, pier flip, then three on the other side. Used to measure CH (collimation) and NP, but only when each side really contributed three stars.
  • Mount error (Mount menu, above Refraction) — you can store a known CH and NP and keep them during a later 2 Stars or PC Alignment session, so those terms stay out of the pole estimate and the two stars re-measure the polar axis.

The numbers shown on the hand controller and web page are Wallace’s familiar names and signs: CH, NP, ID, ME, MA. The pointing itself is not classic linearized TPOINT dial formulas; it is exact rotations (see below).

A bit of math (without the full paper)

A rigid two-axis mount has six geometric degrees of freedom that matter for pointing:

  • Three for the polar axis in space — where the RA axis points (altitude / azimuth of the pole) plus an index about that axis. That is the familiar rotation matrix T from the two-star method.
  • Three for the “head” — CH (optical axis not perpendicular to Dec), NP (RA and Dec axes not perpendicular), ID (Dec index).

Conceptually the chain is:

sky → orientation of the polar axis (T) → head geometry (CH, NP, ID) → instrument pointing.

Two-star (1.5 and still in 1.6): two sky / instrument directions define T. If Mount error is on with non-zero CH/NP, those are held fixed and the two stars still estimate the pole.

Multi-star rigid fit (1.6): more stars are kept (up to nine). The solver starts from the first two (same Taki seed), then refines all six parameters together by minimising the angular residual (a damped Gauss–Newton step on exact rotations). Terms are only published when the star geometry can separate them — for example CH needs both pier sides. That is why 3+3 waits until each side has three stars before showing a CH value.

During a 4-star or 3+3 session, after the third star (and after later stars except the last), the model is updated before the next slew. The hand controller waits while that runs. A mount that starts a long way off therefore gets progressively closer gotos during the same alignment.

One important close-out detail: after a successful rigid fit the firmware does not sync again on the last star. The instrument / encoder frame set by the first accept stays fixed — useful across a pier flip so the stars from the first side remain consistent.

Reported polar misalignment uses the usual Wallace-style packaging: ME from altitude error of the pole, MA related to azimuth; the azimuth tweak on the mount is roughly MA / cos(latitude).

Benefits in practice

  • Better all-sky pointing when the mount has real collimation or axis non-perpendicularity — those errors are measured instead of being forced into the pole.
  • Pier flip: 3+3 is the path that can separate CH; keeping the encoder frame after the fit avoids shifting the first side’s stars.
  • Progressive updates during the session make the later gotos of a long alignment more usable when polar alignment or home was rough.
  • Plate-solve nights: with Mount error holding a known CH/NP, a short 2-star or PC Alignment session can refresh the pole without re-mixing those terms.
  • Exact rotations instead of small-angle dial corrections remain well-behaved when errors are not tiny (near the pole, large polar offset).

What multi-star alignment does not replace

Wrong gear ratio, reversed motor direction, bad site / clock, or a tube that was not at home when you thought it was. If the first goto is degrees off, fix mechanics and home before chasing another model. Residual of several tens of arcminutes after a session is a reason to repeat the procedure, not to hide it with backlash settings.
